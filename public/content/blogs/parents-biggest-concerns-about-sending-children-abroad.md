## What Families Should Check Before Making the Decision 

For a student, studying abroad can look like an opportunity. 

For a parent, it can look like a responsibility spread across thousands of kilometres. 

The student is thinking about the university, course, campus life and career. Parents are often thinking about very different questions: 

**Where will my child live?**

**What if there is an emergency?**

**Can we afford the entire degree?** 

**What happens if the visa rules change?** 

**Will this degree actually help my child build a career?** 

**And if my child needs me, can I even go there?** 

These questions are not signs that parents are against studying abroad. In many cases, they are signs that parents want to understand the decision before making a major financial and emotional commitment. 

The right approach is therefore not to tell parents, **“Don’t worry.”** 

It is to give them enough information to make an informed decision. 

## 1. “Will My Child Be Safe?” 

Safety is usually one of the first concerns when a child moves to another country. 

But asking whether an entire country is “safe” is too broad to be useful. 

Parents should look at the student’s **actual university, city, neighbourhood, accommodation and daily commute**. 

Before finalising a university, families should find out: 

* Where the campus is located 
* How the student will travel between accommodation and campus 
* What accommodation options are available 
* Whether the university has international-student support 
* How students can access emergency assistance 
* here the nearest medical facilities are 
* What transport options are available at the student’s usual travel times 

A university in a major city and a university in a smaller town can provide very different day-to-day experiences. 

So instead of asking: 

**“Is this country safe?”**

ask: 

**“What will my child’s actual daily life look like there?”** 

That is a much more useful safety assessment. 

## 2. “What If My Child Needs Me and I’m Thousands of Kilometres Away?” 

This is one of the hardest parts for parents to accept. 

If a student is sick, loses important documents, faces an accommodation problem or simply feels overwhelmed, the parent cannot be there within an hour. 

That is why students should know their support system **before they leave India**. 

**A useful emergency plan should include:**

* Parent and family contact numbers 
* University emergency contacts 
* Accommodation contact details 
* Local emergency-service information 
* Health-insurance details 
* Copies of important documents 
* Contact details of someone trusted locally, where available

The university’s international office should also be identified before departure. 

The objective is not to prepare for every possible problem. 

It is to make sure that **if a problem occurs, the student knows what to do next.** 

## 3. “Can We Actually Afford the Degree?” 

This is where an overseas education plan needs more than a tuition-fee calculation. 

The real cost can include: 

**Tuition + accommodation + food + transport + insurance + visa expenses + flights + study materials + personal expenses + emergency funds**

And the cost may change depending on the city. 

A student studying in central London, for example, will have a different living-cost profile from someone studying in a smaller UK city. 

The same principle applies to Australia, Canada, the USA, New Zealand and other destinations. 

Parents should therefore calculate the **estimated total cost of the entire programme**, not just the first-year tuition fee. 

**A more important question than “How much loan can we get?”**

Ask: 

**“How much can our family realistically afford to spend and repay?”**

A loan can make an education plan possible, but the repayment obligation does not disappear after graduation. 

Students should also avoid building a financial plan around the assumption that they will definitely earn enough from part-time employment to cover their education. 

Work rights and conditions vary by visa and destination, and employment itself is never guaranteed. 

## 4. “Will This Degree Give My Child a Good Career?” 

A foreign degree is not automatically a career guarantee. 

The value of an overseas education depends on what the student studies, where they study, what skills they develop and how well those skills match the employment market. 

Before choosing a course, parents and students should investigate: 

* Course curriculum 
* Programme duration 
* Internship or placement opportunities 
* Industry exposure 
* Career services 
* Graduate outcomes where reliable data is available 
* Skills developed during the programme 
* Relevant employment opportunities 
* Current post-study work rules 

The question should not simply be: 

**“Is this a famous university?”** 

A better question is: 

**“Is this particular course worth the total investment for this particular student?”**

That shift can prevent families from choosing a university based purely on rankings or reputation. 

## 5. “What If Visa Rules Change?” 

This concern is completely valid. 

Immigration rules are government policies, and policies can change. 

A student planning a three- or four-year education journey should therefore avoid treating today’s immigration rules as a guaranteed promise for the future. 

This is particularly important when families choose a university primarily because of assumptions about: 

* Permanent residence 
* Post-study work 
* Future visa extensions 
* Employment opportunities 
* Dependant eligibility 

The safest approach is to verify immigration information through the **official government website applicable to the student’s visa category.**

A consultant, social-media post or old YouTube video can explain a policy, but the official immigration authority should be the final reference point. 


## 6. “Can Parents Go Abroad With Their Child?” 

This is one of the most important questions parents should ask **before choosing a destination**. 

And there is an important misconception to clear up: 

**Parents are generally not standard dependants of an adult university student.** 

The answer changes when the student is a minor and the country has a specific parent/guardian route. 

Here is how the rules currently work for some of the major study destinations. 

## Australia 

Australia’s Student visa (subclass 500) allows eligible students to include their **partner or dependent children under the specified rules.** Parents are not listed as standard family members who can be included on the Student visa. 

However, Australia has a separate **Student Guardian visa (subclass 590)**. 

A parent, custodian or eligible relative can apply to provide care and support to a student who is **under 18**, or to a student aged 18 or over in exceptional circumstances. The guardian must meet the relevant financial, health and other requirements. 

## What this means: 

**Adult university student:** 
Parent cannot simply be added as a dependant on the Student visa. 

**Eligible student under 18:** 
A parent may be able to accompany the student through the Student Guardian pathway, subject to the visa requirements. 

## United Kingdom 

The UK’s Student visa dependant route is for an eligible student’s **partner or child**, not their parents. Even then, dependant eligibility is restricted to specific student categories. For postgraduate students beginning courses on or after 1 January 2024, the qualifying course generally needs to be a PhD/doctorate or another research-based higher degree, subject to the applicable rules. 

There is a separate **Parent of a Child Student visa**, but it is much narrower than many families assume. 

It applies where the child has or is applying for a Child Student visa, and the child must be **aged 4 to 11 and attending an independent school in the UK.**

Therefore, this is **not a route for a parent to live in the UK with an adult child doing a bachelor’s or master’s degree**. 

Parents can, however, visit their child through the appropriate visitor route if they meet the requirements. The UK’s Standard Visitor route allows visits to see family or friends, normally for up to six months, subject to the visitor rules. 

## New Zealand 

New Zealand has one of the clearest parent/guardian provisions for younger students. 

Its **Guardian Visitor Visa** allows a parent or legal guardian to live with and care for a child who has a current New Zealand Student Visa. 

The child must be **17 or younger, or enrolled in school years 1 to 13**. Only one parent or legal guardian can hold this visa at a time. 

This is therefore a genuine parent-accompaniment option for eligible school-age students. 

It is **not a general route for parents to move to New Zealand with an adult university student.** 

Parents of adult students may still visit under an applicable visitor route if they meet the requirements. New Zealand’s Visitor Visa permits visits to family and friends, subject to its eligibility and stay conditions. 

## Canada 

Canada’s study-permit family provisions focus on the student’s **spouse/common-law partner and dependent children**, who must make their own applications and meet the applicable requirements. Parents are not listed as standard dependants of an international student. 

A parent who wants to visit Canada generally needs to qualify under the applicable visitor route. 

There is also a **Parent and Grandparent Super Visa**, but this should not be confused with a general parent-dependant route for international students. The Super Visa is designed for parents and grandparents of **Canadian citizens or permanent residents** and has its own eligibility requirements. 

So a student’s parents should not assume that their child’s Canadian study permit gives them a right to live in Canada. 

## USA 

The US student-visa system is also different from the idea of parents being dependants. 

The US Department of State identifies F and M visas as student visa categories and separately provides visitor visas for tourism and visits. 

A parent visiting an adult student would generally need to qualify for the appropriate **visitor status**, rather than being added to the student’s F-1 visa as a dependant. 

For a temporary family visit, the relevant visitor category is generally the **B-2 or combined B1/B2 visitor visa**, subject to the normal eligibility requirements. 

**So, Can Parents Stay With Their Child for the Whole Degree?** 

For an **adult university student**, families should not assume that they can. 

The possibility of a parent living with a student depends on the country’s immigration system and the student’s age and visa category. 

The clearest parent/guardian arrangements among the destinations discussed above are aimed primarily at **minor school-age students**, not adults pursuing university education. 

For an adult student, parents may instead be able to **visit** under the destination’s visitor rules, provided they qualify. 

That distinction is extremely important: 

**“Can my parent visit me?” ≠ “Can my parent live with me throughout my degree?”**

They are two different immigration questions. 

## 7. “What If My Child Cannot Manage Living Alone?”

Academic preparation does not automatically mean practical independence. 

A student may know how to write assignments and attend lectures but still have little experience with: 

* Monthly budgeting 
* Grocery shopping 
* Cooking 
* Laundry 
* Public transport 
* Booking appointments 
* Managing documents 
* Handling a bank account 
* Making decisions without parents 

These are small responsibilities individually. 

Together, they become a major part of everyday student life. 

Parents can make the transition easier by giving their child responsibility **before departure**. 

Let them manage a monthly budget. 

Ask them to plan their own travel. 

Teach them basic cooking. 

Make sure they know where their important documents are. 

The objective is not perfection. 

It is familiarity. 

## 8. “What If My Child Feels Lonely?” 

A student can be excited about going abroad and still miss home. 

Both things can be true at the same time. 

The first few weeks can involve unfamiliar food, new teaching methods, a different social environment and the absence of the family routine they have known for years. 

Parents can help without turning every day into a check-in session. 

Instead of constantly asking: 

**“Are you okay?”** 

families can agree on a communication routine before departure. 

For example: 

* Short regular calls 
* A longer family conversation once a week 
* Immediate communication in case of an emergency 

This gives the student emotional support while still allowing them to develop independence. 

## 9. “What If My Child Struggles Academically?”

The teaching style abroad may not feel identical to what the student experienced in India. 

Depending on the course and university, students may encounter greater emphasis on: 

* Independent study 
* Research 
* Presentations 
* Group projects 
* Academic writing 
* Critical analysis 
* Referencing 

Students should therefore identify the university’s academic support services before arriving. 

The important question is not: 

**“Will my child ever struggle?”**

They might. 

The more useful question is: 

**“Does my child know where to get help if they do?”**

## 10. “What If My Child Cannot Find a Job After Graduation?” 

No responsible study-abroad plan should promise a job. 

Employment depends on multiple factors, including the student’s: 

* Qualification 
* Skills 
* Work experience 
* English or local-language ability where relevant 
* Networking 
* Industry demand 
* Location 
* Work authorisation 
* Overall labour-market conditions 

Students should start building employability during their studies rather than waiting until the final semester. 

Internships, projects, networking, relevant skills and career-services support can all contribute to a stronger profile. 

The realistic formula is: 

**Education + Skills + Experience + Job Search = Better Employability** 

Not: 

**Foreign Degree = Guaranteed Job** 

## 11. “What If Something Goes Wrong With Accommodation?” 

Accommodation is often treated as an afterthought. 

It shouldn’t be. 

Before paying for accommodation, students should understand: 

* Exact location 
* Distance from campus 
* Transport options 
* Contract duration 
* Deposit requirements 
* Cancellation/refund terms 
* What utilities are included 
* Whether the accommodation is university-managed or private 
* Who handles maintenance or emergencies

Parents should also keep a copy of the accommodation agreement and the student’s address. 

A student knowing exactly where they are staying is basic preparation not unnecessary paperwork. 

## 12. “Should We Choose a Country Because of PR?” 

This is one of the most dangerous ways to simplify a study-abroad decision. 

Immigration pathways can change. 

Eligibility can depend on the student’s qualification, occupation, work experience, location, visa status and other factors. 

A student should not spend several years and a significant amount of money on a course solely because someone said: 

**“This course will definitely give you PR.”** 

Instead, families should evaluate the course independently of any future immigration outcome. 

If a post-study immigration pathway is important to the family, its current official requirements should be checked separately. 

## 13. “What If the Student Gets Sick Abroad?” 

Health planning should happen before departure. 

Students should know: 

* What health insurance or healthcare coverage applies to them 
* How to access medical services 
* What their university’s support system provides 
* Where to find emergency assistance 
* Which documents they need for healthcare 
* How their family can contact them in an emergency 

Parents should keep copies of important information while the student keeps their own accessible copies abroad. 

The exact healthcare system and insurance requirements vary by country and visa. 

**A Parent’s Pre-Departure Reality Check**

Before saying yes to an overseas education plan, parents and students should be able to answer these questions: 

**Academic** 

Why this course? 

**Financial** 

What is the estimated total cost not just tuition? 

**Safety** 

Where exactly will the student live and how will they commute? 

**Support** 

Who can the student contact if something goes wrong? 

**Career** 

What skills and career opportunities does this course realistically offer? 

**Immigration** 

What are the current visa conditions and post-study options? 

**Family** 

Can parents visit? Can they legally accompany the student? Or is a separate visa required? 

**Backup** 

What happens if the student’s first plan does not work? 

If a family can answer these questions clearly, the decision becomes far more structured. 

## FAQs:

**1.Can parents accompany a child studying abroad?** 

It depends on the student’s age, destination and visa category. 

For adult university students, parents are generally not included as standard dependants. Some countries have specific guardian provisions for minor students. 

**2.Which countries allow parents to accompany minor students?** 

Examples include Australia and New Zealand, which have specific guardian routes for eligible younger students. The UK also has a Parent of a Child Student route, but it applies to a much narrower category: a child aged 4-11 attending an independent school. 

The exact requirements should always be checked for the student’s visa category. 

**3.Can parents accompany an adult student doing a bachelor’s degree?** 

Generally, parents should not assume they can live in the destination country as dependants of an adult bachelor’s student. 

In countries such as Australia, the standard Student visa family provisions cover a partner and eligible dependent children not parents. 

Parents may instead qualify to visit under a separate visitor route. 

**4.Can parents visit their child while they study abroad?**

Potentially, yes, depending on the destination and the parent’s eligibility. 

For example, the UK allows Standard Visitors to visit family and friends, while the US has visitor visa categories that cover tourism and visits to family and friends. New Zealand also has visitor routes for visiting family and friends. 

A student’s visa does not automatically guarantee approval of a parent’s visitor application. 

**5.Should parents choose a country based only on PR opportunities?** 

No. 

PR policies can change, and eligibility is individual. The course should make sense academically and financially even when future immigration outcomes are uncertain. 

## Important Note 

Immigration rules, visa conditions, fees and eligibility criteria can change. The information in this article reflects the official government information available at the time of writing **(August 2026)** and should be rechecked on the relevant government website before making an application or financial commitment.
